AI Alchemy · Skill test #1 · archify

I asked an AI to explain my pizza order. It drew me three maps.

Archify is an agent skill that turns a plain-language description into an interactive, self-checking diagram. I installed it in a clean sandbox, fed it a pizza delivery app, and kept poking until it stopped yelling at me.

The short version

  • Archify makes really good looking diagrams you can click around in: guided tours, "how do I get from A to B" routes, four visual styles, dark and light.
  • The magic isn't the drawing. It's the strict validator that refuses to ship overlapping labels or pages that don't fit the screen, and tells the agent exactly how to fix them.
  • It is a complicated skill. Expect your agent to go back and forth several times. That's the skill working, not failing.

Wait, what is Archify?

Most "draw me a diagram" tools ask the AI to spit out Mermaid code or raw SVG and hope for the best. Archify takes a different route. Your agent writes a small JSON recipe (boxes, arrows, labels), and a bundled Node tool turns that recipe into one standalone HTML file with the diagram baked in.

It speaks five diagram types: architecture (what talks to what), workflow (steps and approvals), sequence (who calls whom, in order), dataflow (pipelines), and lifecycle (states, like an order status). It can also read Mermaid you paste in. The repo claims it hit #1 on GitHub's weekly trending list in September 2026, so plenty of people are curious.

Here's the loop the skill tells the agent to follow. The key part is the red arrow going backwards.

Setting up the kitchen

I cloned the repo into an empty sandbox and copied only the skill folder into a fresh directory, so nothing else on the machine could help or interfere. The good news: zero dependencies. No npm install. Just Node 18+.

One gotcha. The browser check step quietly skips itself if it can't find Chrome. In my sandbox I had to point it at Chromium with ARCHIFY_CHROME=/path/to/chrome. If your agent says "visual check skipped", that's why.

For the demo I picked something everyone has used and nobody has seen the inside of: a pizza delivery app. I invented a small one called PizzaNow and drew it three ways.

Diagram 1: the big map

First, an architecture diagram. What boxes exist behind the "Order" button, and who talks to whom? Twelve components: your phone, a gateway, the order service (the brain), the database, payments, a card processor, the kitchen tablet, an event bus, dispatch, maps, the courier app, and push notifications.

My first try failed validation in about 60 milliseconds. What surprised me was how it failed:

Label "save" overlaps component "orders"
  label rect: [510, 340, 30, 14]
  component "orders" rect: [460, 300, 130, 60]
  Suggested fix: labelAt [525, 374] or labelDy +24 (below)

It doesn't just say "something's off". It gives the exact rectangle, the exact thing it bumped into, and two ready-made fixes. That's the kind of error message an AI agent can act on without guessing. Here's my whole build log for this one:

TICKET · architecture12 boxes · 11 arrows
  1. R12 labels sitting on top of boxes. Applied the suggested labelDy: 24.
  2. R2Validator passed 9/9. Delivered. Then the Chrome check failed: the page was 1204px tall on a 900px laptop screen.
  3. R3Looking at the screenshot, I also saw my "cloud" box had swallowed Stripe and Maps (boundaries are drawn around everything in their area). Moved the partners outside and packed rows tighter. Two label bumps.
  4. R4Passed 9/9 checks, 0 warnings, fits at 1440×900 and 2048×1320 in both themes.
4 roundsSERVED

Round 3 is the interesting one. The validator can't know that Stripe shouldn't live inside my cloud. Only a human (or an agent that actually looks at the screenshot) catches that. Archify's docs are refreshingly upfront about this: the automated checks prove layout, and "perceptual review requires an actual human or image-capable reviewer."

Same system, but now it's a story in time: you tap, the app posts the order, payment gets approved, the kitchen gets a ticket, dispatch finds a courier, and eventually, ding dong. The sequence view also shrinks the text more than the other types. The subtitles ("very hungry", "on your phone") are hard to read at laptop size. You can zoom in, but it's worth knowing before you put one in a slide.

Diagram 3: where's my pizza?

For the last one I tried the Mermaid route. I wrote a quick stateDiagram of order statuses, the same way you might sketch one in a README, and asked for a lifecycle diagram from it:

stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Placed --> Paying --> Cooking --> OnTheRoad --> Delivered
    Cooking --> OvenQueue : kitchen slammed
    OnTheRoad --> WrongAddress : can't find door
    WrongAddress --> OnTheRoad : customer calls back
    OvenQueue --> Cancelled : waited 60 min
    WrongAddress --> Refunded : gave up

Archify doesn't auto-convert Mermaid. The skill tells the agent to read it for meaning and write a fresh spec, which lets it put things in "lanes": the happy path on top, detours in the middle, sad endings at the bottom.

This small diagram took the most rounds, and it taught me two sneaky lessons:

TICKET · lifecycle9 states · 5 detours
  1. R120 errors at once. Turns out lane names are magic: my bottom lane was called end, but Archify only treats a lane named terminal as its own row.
  2. R2Renamed lanes. Down to 5 errors: 4 labels on boxes, plus two labels stacked on each other.
  3. R3The two arrows between "On the Road" and "Wrong Address" run side by side, 14px apart. Their labels collided.
  4. R4Nudged one label left, one right. Validator passed. Chrome: 1px too tall. One pixel!
  5. R5Shrank the canvas. The bottom row fell outside the drawing area.
  6. R6Split the difference. Still off the bottom.
  7. R7Real culprit: my title "Where's My Pizza? Every Order Status" was wrapping onto two lines. Shortened it. Passed everything.
7 roundsSERVED

A one-pixel overflow sounds petty, but that strictness is the point. Archify's rule is that the diagram plus its summary cards should fit on one laptop screen with no scrolling, and it will not fake a pass by hiding overflow.

The fun part: clicking around

Every diagram comes with a reader toolkit built in. I didn't write any of this; it's in every file Archify produces.

Four outfits, one diagram

A dropdown swaps the visual preset. Same boxes and arrows, different mood. Classic is the default; the others are for when you want to show off.

The Export menu also saves PNG, SVG, WebP, JPEG, a social "share card", or even a WebM video of the trace animation. Each HTML file is about 800 KB because the fonts ride along inside it. That's why it works offline, and why you can email it to a friend who has never heard of Archify.

Fifteen rounds for three diagrams. Each check runs in well under a second and the browser check in about five, so it's fast. You just need an agent patient enough to keep going. The skill actually tells the agent when to give up: if two rounds in a row don't lower the error count, stop and report honestly.

Tips if you try it

  1. Let the agent drive. Say "use Archify to diagram X" and let it loop. Don't panic at red rounds; that's the recipe.
  2. Pick the type by your question. "What exists?" is architecture. "What happens in order?" is sequence. "What status can it be in?" is lifecycle.
  3. Keep titles short. A title that wraps to two lines can push the page over the fit-on-one-screen rule.
  4. Add 2 to 4 guided views. One line each. It's the cheapest way to turn a diagram into a story someone can click through.
  5. Look at the screenshot yourself. The checks catch overlaps, not nonsense. Only you know if Stripe belongs inside your cloud.
  6. Have Chrome available (or set ARCHIFY_CHROME) so the fit-on-screen check actually runs.

Verdict

Archify is less "draw me a picture" and more "a picky art director with a ruler". That makes it slower than a quick Mermaid sketch, but what comes out the other end is a diagram you'd happily drop into a design doc, a pitch deck, or a blog post like this one. If you explain systems to people for a living, or just like pretty maps of how things work, it's worth an afternoon.
