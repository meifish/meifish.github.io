Every streak is a crumb of Halley's Comet

Earth drives through the comet's old dust trail every October.

Halley's Comet only swings by every 76 years, but it leaves grit behind on each lap. Around October 21, our planet ploughs through that trail and the grains, most smaller than a grain of sand, burn up high in the air. That flash is a meteor.

  • ~20 / hrin a typical year, under a properly dark sky. Some years more, some fewer.
  • 66 km/sabout 148,000 mph. Orionids are among the fastest meteors, so they're quick, bright and often leave a glowing trail.
  • Orion's clubis where they seem to come from, near the red star Betelgeuse. You don't need to stare there; meteors cross the whole sky.

The night, hour by hour

  1. 8 pm

    Gates open

    Park at the North Meadow lot and claim a patch of grass. Headlights off once you're in; the meadow is a red-light-only zone.

  2. 10:30

    Orion clears the trees

    The shower's radiant rises in the east. Rangers walk the meadow with a laser pointer to show you where to look and how to count.

  3. 1 am

    Moonset

    The moon drops below the hills. Your eyes have had hours to adjust, and fainter meteors start to show.

  4. 2–5 am

    The good part

    Orion is high and the sky is at its darkest. This is when most meteors fall. Lie flat, look about halfway up, and be patient.

  5. 6:15

    First light

    Dawn washes the sky. Last cocoa, fold the blanket, drive home slowly.

Times are approximate for a mid-northern site. Clouds can win; the weather plan will be announced closer to the night, and people who RSVP hear first.

Pack like it's January

You'll be lying still for hours. October nights get colder than you think.

  • Warm layers

    Hat, gloves, a coat you'd wear in winter.

  • A recliner or blanket

    Flat on your back beats craning your neck.

  • A red flashlight

    Or tape red cellophane over a normal one.

  • A thermos

    Rangers bring cocoa. Bring your own top-up.
